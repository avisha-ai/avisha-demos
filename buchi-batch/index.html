Spotting the batch that heated and cooled more slowly

Demo with illustrative, simulated data. Not real plant data, not a statement about any equipment or supplier.

Repeat batches of the same recipe in the same jacketed reactor should heat up and cool down alike. When one of them takes longer, that is the batch an engineer should look at first.

Below: ten simulated batches of one recipe: heat up to 80 °C, hold 45 minutes, cool down to 30 °C. Nine agree with each other. One does not.

2. Time to reach the set point

Flag: Batch 7

What changed, when, and by how much

  • Heat-up: reached 80 °C after 72 minutes, against 54–57 minutes for the normal batches (about 29% longer). The difference shows from the first minutes of heating.
  • Cool-down: took 66 minutes from 80 °C to 30 °C, against 50–52 minutes (about 30% longer).
  • Whole batch: 183 minutes, against 148–154 minutes. The start temperature (21.0 °C) and the jacket settings were the same as for the other batches.

Possible reasons an engineer would check: deposits on the vessel wall, jacket fluid flow or temperature, batch size or fill level, stirring, or the recipe.

A flag shows what changed. It does not establish why.

How the data was made

Simulated, illustrative only. A simple energy balance of one jacketed reactor: about 80 kg of a water-like batch, heat transfer through the jacket of about 150 W/K (illustrative), small batch-to-batch differences (start 19–21 °C, heat transfer ±3%, batch size ±1%). The jacket is set to 100 °C for heating, 80 °C for the hold and 10 °C for cooling, and follows its set value with a lag of about 4 minutes. Batch 7 has about 25% less heat transfer, as a fouled wall might cause. No real reactor, recipe or plant is represented.
